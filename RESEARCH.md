# Research basis for the course

What the evidence says about learning a language by reading, and what each finding
means for how readers are written. When a decision about the course is unclear, check
it against this file. Update it when new evidence changes a decision.

## 1. Comprehensible, compelling input (Krashen)

**Claims.** Krashen's comprehension (input) hypothesis: we acquire language in one way,
by understanding messages - comprehensible input slightly beyond the current level
("i+1"). His later *compelling input* hypothesis: the most effective input is so
interesting that the reader forgets it is another language (Lao & Krashen 2014;
Krashen 2011). Supporting ideas:
- **Free voluntary reading:** self-selected pleasure reading, usually fiction, is the
  most powerful form of input for reaching advanced levels.
- **Narrow reading:** good readers stick to one author, genre or series at a time, which
  recycles vocabulary and context naturally.
- **Affective filter:** anxiety, boredom and pressure block acquisition.
- **Acquisition vs learning:** consciously learned rules have limited use; acquired
  language comes from input. Speaking emerges from input; it is not the cause.

**Criticism.** Influential but contested: the constructs (i+1, acquisition vs learning)
are vague and hard to test, the strong claim that conscious learning cannot become
acquisition is not supported, and meta-analyses find explicit grammar instruction does
help (see 4). Recent critiques add that interaction, feedback and output also matter.

**What we take.** Input-first, as Krashen says - but not input-*only*:
- Stories must be **compelling**: real tales with plot, suspense, humour and darkness,
  never filler. Interest is a design requirement, not decoration.
- **Adam chooses** what comes next where possible (self-selected reading): offer 2-3
  options for the next story or thread each batch.
- **Narrow reading:** serials (Hloupý Honza, Pejsek a kočička) and author runs (Erben,
  Čapek) rather than unrelated one-offs.
- **Low pressure:** no tests, no drills, no quotas. Adam burned out on books that were
  too hard - enjoyment and flow protect the habit, and the habit is what gets results.
- **Pleasure reading outside the course counts too:** encourage anything comprehensible
  he enjoys.

## 2. Lexical coverage: how much must be known

- 95% of word tokens known = minimal comprehension; **98% = comfortable, independent
  reading** (Hu & Nation 2000; Laufer & Ravenhorst-Kalovski 2010; Laufer 2021).
- A 2025 replication (Webb, Pellicer-Sánchez & Wang) found comprehension rising from
  90% to 100% but no significant differences between 90/95/98% for advanced learners -
  coverage matters, but is not the only factor.

**Rule:** >= 95% of tokens core / taught / half-known, aiming for ~98%; at most ~5% new.

## 3. Repetition: how often a word must be met

- Words met while reading are learned reliably after roughly **7-10 encounters**, with
  gains flattening after ~20 (Uchihara, Webb & Yanagisawa 2019; Springer 2025 study).
- Each single encounter teaches little: incidental gains average ~15% across reading,
  listening and reading-while-listening (Webb, Uchihara & Yanagisawa 2023 meta-analysis).
- Varied contexts help: the same word in different stories and situations.

**Rule:** each new word gets 3-5 uses in its first reader, then is recycled to ~10-20
total encounters across the next ~10 readers, in different stories. Volume and rereading
matter more than any single reader.

## 4. Grammar: explicit plus input

- Explicit instruction is more effective than purely implicit exposure (Norris & Ortega
  2000: large vs medium effects; Spada & Tomita 2010: explicit advantage for simple and
  complex features). Combining explicit attention with lots of input appears best.
- Explicit help works best for small, clear forms (an ending, a tense marker).

**Rule:** a new form arrives inside stories with **one short note** (in the LingQ lesson
description or in chat - never in the reader text), then hundreds of examples. No tables to memorise, no drills (this also respects Krashen's
point about the limits of conscious rules and the affective filter).

## 5. Output

Krashen sees speaking as a result of input; Swain's output hypothesis and the critiques
in section 1 argue that producing language helps learners notice gaps.

**Rule:** output stays optional and light - a 2-3 sentence summary after a reader if
Adam wants, corrected gently, with errors fed back into the next readers.

## 6. What this means for each batch (checklist)

1. Is every reader a story worth reading for itself? (compelling)
2. Did Adam get a say in what comes next? (self-selected)
3. Coverage >= 95%, aiming 98%? (`scripts/check.py`)
4. Are earlier new words coming back often enough to reach 10-20 encounters? (ledger)
5. Is any new grammar noted briefly (lesson description / chat, not in the text) and then
   used constantly?
6. Does it read as relaxed and enjoyable, not like a lesson? (affective filter)
